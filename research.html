From Deferral to Learning: Online In-Context Knowledge Distillation for LLM Cascades

Yu Wu*, Shuo Wu*, Ye Tao*, Yansong Li, Anand D. Sarwate
* equal contribution

TMLR 2026 arXiv

Powerful language models cost money every time you ask. This teaches a cheaper one to handle more questions itself.

Cascades save money by letting a cheap model answer easy questions and passing the hard ones up to an expensive model. But the arrangement is static: when the strong model solves a problem its reasoning is thrown away, so the next near-identical question costs full price all over again. Inter-Cascade asks the strong model to also write down a generalized strategy for problems of that kind, which is stored and retrieved by similarity to augment the weak model's context on later queries. The weak model improves during use, with no fine-tuning. On GSM-Symbolic its accuracy rose from 15.04% to 48.10% against a calibrated-cascade baseline, while calls to the strong model fell 48.05% and token cost fell 49.63%. The gain is not free — feeding strategies in roughly doubles the weak model's own token use — and an ablation shows randomly chosen strategies help far less than retrieved ones.

Attention is the Address, Values are the Message: Zero-Shot Reranking Below the Softmax

Shuo Wu
sole author

under review, ICLR 2027

A language model already knows which retrieved documents matter. This reads that signal out of its attention in one pass.

Attention-based rerankers score each retrieved document by how much attention the query puts on it, then need a second "calibration" forward pass to cancel the model's built-in bias toward some tokens. I show that these methods share one formula and differ only in where they read attention, never in how they score it. AV-IP changes the scoring itself: it compares the query's and the document's value vectors, and centering that score provably does the calibration pass's job, so the second pass is no longer needed. Across two frozen 7–8B models and eight BEIR datasets, even the weakest AV-IP variant beats the strongest attention-mass baseline on macro nDCG@10. The margin is small (about +0.005), but its per-query bootstrap confidence interval excludes zero on both models. Dropping the baselines' calibration pass would save them 30–49% latency at a cost of 3.7–4.2 nDCG points; AV-IP never pays it.

Expert Advice with Costly Observations

Lev Reyzin, Aadirupa Saha, Shuo Wu

UAI 2026 (accepted) Proceedings forthcoming (PMLR), conference Aug 17–21 2026 link to follow

When asking each expert costs money, how many should you consult before you decide?

Online learning normally assumes you see every expert's advice for free. In crowdsourcing, and in the human-feedback pipelines used to train language models, you pay for each opinion, and experts differ in both accuracy and price. We study the version where each round the learner chooses which experts to pay to observe and then which one of them to follow, judged against the best fixed expert in hindsight — who also pays its own observation costs. We give two algorithms, one for adversarially chosen losses and one for the stochastic case, together with information-theoretic lower bounds that match them. What emerges is a single tradeoff with three regimes: when opinions are expensive the optimal strategy consults one expert per round and behaves like a bandit; when they are cheap it consults everyone and recovers full-information learning; in between it consults an intermediate number that the analysis pins down exactly.

Author order is alphabetical.

Robust Reward Design for Markov Decision Processes

Shuo Wu, Haoxiang Ma, Jie Fu, Shuo Han

JAIR vol. 84, 2025 Journal article preprint

If you design rewards to steer someone's behavior, small mistakes about them can ruin the plan.

A leader can steer a follower's behavior by modifying the follower's reward function — a defender shaping how an attacker values targets on a network, for instance. Standard methods assume the leader models the follower exactly, and they degrade in three distinct ways: when the follower's best response is not unique and it breaks ties unfavorably, when the leader's knowledge of the follower's rewards is imprecise, and when the follower is not perfectly rational. We show that robustness to all three follows from one geometric choice — take an allocation lying strictly inside the region of optimal solutions rather than on its boundary. Such an interior point is guaranteed to exist when the leader can reach its best payoff without exhausting its budget, and one can be computed with a mixed-integer linear program. On an attack-graph benchmark the leader's payoff holds at 0.655 under adversarial tie-breaking, where the standard solution falls to 0.248.

Observational Learning with a Budget

Shuo Wu, Pawan Poojary, Randall Berry

IEEE ISIT 2025 preprint

People copy each other's choices. With a limited budget, whose information should you improve so crowds get it right?

When people decide one after another and can see what earlier people chose, there comes a point where copying is the rational move and no one's private information reaches the crowd any more. That cascade is permanent once it starts, and it can settle on the wrong answer. We ask where a planner with a fixed budget should spend it to make individuals' private signals more informative. Unlike earlier models we let the two error rates differ, which turns the history of decisions into a random walk with asymmetric step sizes between two absorbing boundaries, and from that we derive closed-form probabilities of reaching a correct cascade. The budget question then collapses to two candidate allocations — pour everything into the weaker signal, or level the two — and we show at least one of them is always optimal. Spending the whole budget can be strictly worse than spending only part of it.

Margin-Based Sparsification for Feature-Budgeted Linear SVM

Lev Reyzin, Shuo Wu

ISAIM 2026 (accepted) PDF accepted papers

Which handful of measurements can you keep and still classify almost as well as using all of them?

A trained linear classifier normally assigns a weight to every feature, so the cost of each prediction scales with all of them. Forcing it down to a fixed budget of K features is awkward in practice: penalizing the weights controls sparsity only indirectly, so many values of K turn out to be unreachable, while eliminating features greedily one at a time is expensive and lets an early mistake propagate through everything that follows. We instead sample coordinates in proportion to the size of their weights and rescale, which gives a K-feature classifier that is unbiased — its margin matches the dense classifier's in expectation — and a short round of repetition turns that into a guarantee for a single fixed classifier. On two image benchmarks the method reaches 94–99% accuracy using only 3–8 features, and at the smallest budgets its confidence intervals are up to 14.73 times tighter than recursive feature elimination.

When to Trust Your Data: Enhancing Dyna-Style Model-Based Reinforcement Learning With Data Filter

Yansong Li, Zeyu Dong, Ertai Luo, Yu Wu, Shuo Wu, Shuo Han

preprint arXiv

A robot learning from an imagined world should ignore the parts it imagined badly.

Model-based reinforcement learning speeds up training by generating extra experience from a learned model of the environment instead of collecting it for real. When that learned model is inaccurate the imagined experience works against the learner, and the standard remedies — training an ensemble of models, or pretraining on additional real data — buy reliability with more computation and more real-world interaction. We add a filter that simply discards imagined transitions lying far from anything actually observed, keeping only the ones the model is likely to have got right, and show theoretically that this bounds how far the resulting value updates can drift from the updates real data would have produced. Built into a standard Dyna-style algorithm and tested on three continuous-control tasks, it reaches the same level of performance with fewer real interactions — and does so without the ensemble that made the baseline expensive.
